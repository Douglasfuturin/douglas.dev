# Voice — Operator Voice for Spoken VSLs

A VSL is **spoken**, which means the voice rules are different from written long-form copy. The lines have to read out loud naturally, with breath marks where a human would actually pause, and without phrasings that sound fine on paper but trip the tongue on camera.

## The single biggest rule: anti-staccato

VSLs that read like a string of short period-terminated sentences sound robotic on camera. The visitor's ear immediately registers "this is a script being read" rather than "this is a person talking to me." Real operators don't talk in three-word bursts — they think in clauses, link them with commas and em-dashes, and only land a period when a thought genuinely ends.

### Before (AI-staccato — never ship this)
> Meta Ads in one tab. QuickBooks in another. Hyros in a third. Close, Whop, ActiveCampaign, Slack, Airtable. Every question that crosses two of them is a 40-minute scavenger hunt.

### After (operator voice — ship this)
> Meta Ads sits in one tab, QuickBooks in another, Hyros in a third, and on top of all that you've got Close, Whop, ActiveCampaign, Slack, and Airtable — so every question that crosses two of them turns into a forty-minute scavenger hunt where the answer goes stale the moment you stop looking.

One flowing sentence with commas, em-dashes, and a stretch of momentum. Same information, completely different read on camera.

## Oxford commas, every time

"Strategy, execution, and review" — not "strategy, execution and review". This is non-negotiable for premium B2B voice. Without the Oxford comma, lists feel rushed and slightly off; with it, they land with intent.

## Numbers spelled out where they breathe better

In writing: "12 weeks" reads cleaner.
In speech: **"twelve weeks"** reads cleaner because the eye doesn't skip past it the way it skips past "12".

Spell out numbers under twenty in VSL scripts. Keep large or specific numbers in numerals ($47,283, 4,217 founders) because those benefit from the visual weight.

| Context | Use |
|---------|-----|
| Time durations | "twelve weeks", "ninety days", "thirty-day guarantee" |
| Small counts | "three pillars", "seven assistants", "ten roles" |
| Dollar amounts | $4,997, $18-24k, $47M |
| Percentages | "20%", "ninety percent" (either works; pick one and be consistent) |

## Transitional phrases — the connective tissue

These phrases are what make a VSL feel like a conversation. Use them between beats, before pivots, and before objection handling. Don't overuse — one or two per beat keeps the voice warm without templating it.

| Phrase | When to use |
|--------|-------------|
| "And look,..." | Before a softer beat, a vulnerability moment, or a disqualifier |
| "Now look,..." | Before a pivot or a redirect mid-sentence |
| "Here's the thing:..." | Before a key insight or the mechanism reveal |
| "Now you might be thinking..." | Opening any objection-handling beat |
| "Let me be clear about something..." | Before a disqualifier or an honest beat ("this isn't for everyone") |
| "Here's what's actually happening:..." | Before a contrarian reframe or the identity-crisis beat |
| "Now I get it, ..." | Validating a concern before answering it |
| "But here's what most people don't realize:..." | Before mechanism reveal or hidden-insight beat |
| "So here's exactly what we do:" | Before the offer reveal or the how-it-works beat |
| "Now to be clear:..." | Before clarifying scope or guarantees |

## Repetition for rhythm

Real spoken VSLs use intentional repetition to create cadence. Watch for these patterns:

**Triplet repetition** (most common):
> "You're working too much. You're tired. You're missing having an actual life."

> "We have the best people. Best thumbnail designers. Best editors. Best content writers. Best strategists. We have the best people."

**Inverted repetition** (frames the same idea two ways):
> "More clients = more work = more hours = no freedom."

**Anaphora** (same opening, different completions):
> "Your designers can integrate the outputs. Your copywriters can refine the copy. Your strategists can produce everything they need."

Repetition lands on camera because it creates emphasis without raising volume. Use 1-2 repetitions per beat — more than that feels overwritten.

## The "you" frequency

A high-converting VSL says "you" / "your" approximately every 1-2 sentences. The visitor needs to feel addressed personally throughout, not just at the opening and the CTA.

### Before
> Most agencies struggle with creative production. Tools don't talk to each other. The system breaks down.

### After
> Most agencies in your position struggle with creative production. The tools you're using don't talk to each other. The system you've cobbled together breaks down.

Same beat, but the visitor now feels named.

## Contractions, always

Spoken VSLs use contractions because that's how humans talk:
- "you're" not "you are"
- "it's" not "it is"
- "we'll" not "we will"
- "I'm" not "I am"
- "don't" not "do not"

The one exception: if you're landing a hard emphasis on a contradiction or a refusal, the uncontracted form lands harder. "We do not stop until you get there" hits harder than "we don't stop." Use sparingly.

## Sentence rhythm

Vary sentence length deliberately. After 2-3 long flowing sentences, drop in one short punchy one — this is where the period earns its keep.

### Example
> "Most agencies approach this the wrong way: they think they just need better prompts, or more time learning AI tools, or some new tool nobody's discovered yet that's better than all the others. But let me tell you this completely directly. **That is not the issue.** The issue is that you need workflows, you need processes, you need AI agents, you need an entire system."

The short sentence in the middle ("That is not the issue.") is what gives the long ones around it room to breathe. Without short sentences for contrast, long sentences feel relentless. Without long sentences for momentum, short sentences feel choppy.

## Words and phrases to avoid on camera

These are written-copy moves that don't survive being spoken out loud:

| Don't say | Why |
|-----------|-----|
| "Furthermore" | Nobody talks like this |
| "In conclusion" | Doesn't belong in a VSL at all |
| "Various" / "numerous" | Lazy quantifiers — be specific or cut |
| "It is important to note that..." | Throat-clearing |
| "I would like to share with you..." | Throat-clearing |
| "Let me ask you a question..." | Rhetorical questions hooks read as filler |
| "At the end of the day..." | Cliché, signals lazy thinking |
| "Synergy" / "leverage" (as verb) | Corporate-buzzword tells |
| "Game-changer" / "next-level" | Empty intensifiers |
| "Literally" (as intensifier) | Feels like a tic |
| "Honestly..." (as a tic) | Use once at most per VSL |

## Words and phrases to USE on camera

| Do say | Why |
|--------|-----|
| "Here's the thing:..." | Strong pivot, sounds like real conversation |
| "And look,..." | Operator voice tag |
| "Let me be clear:..." | Hardens an honest beat |
| "Right now..." | Pulls the visitor into the present moment |
| "Even if [doubt], you can still [outcome]" | Pre-empts skepticism elegantly |
| "Because [reason]" | Justifies a claim; specifically powerful word per Robert Cialdini |
| "What if I told you..." | Classic hook opener |
| "Here's what's actually happening:" | Contrarian reframe lead-in |

## The read-out-loud test

Before delivering ANY VSL script, read it out loud at normal pace. The lines that trip your tongue, force you to take an awkward breath, or sound stilted are the lines that need rewriting. If you can't say it smoothly, the founder can't either, and the audience will hear it.

Specifically catch:
- Sentences that need a breath mid-clause (split with em-dash or comma)
- Words you stumble on (replace with a synonym you don't stumble on)
- Phrases that sound corporate, written, or salesy (rewrite in the voice of someone explaining the thing to a friend over coffee)

## Pacing markers in the script

Drop these into the script in italics so the founder knows how to read it:

- `[pause]` — full second of silence after a sentence
- `[hold beat]` — slightly longer pause for emphasis (2 seconds)
- `[smile here]` — non-verbal moment, usually after the hook or before the reframe
- `[hit harder]` — emphasis on the next line (the founder will know what to do)
- `[look at camera]` — break the read, look at the lens, then continue
- `[slow down]` — applies to the next sentence
- `[say faster]` — applies when listing 3+ things in a row

Don't over-direct. 5-10 pacing markers across a 10-minute VSL is plenty. More than that and the founder is reading directions instead of pitching.
