# Copy Patterns

Proven formulas for the parts of the page that do the heaviest lifting. Adapt — don't copy verbatim — to the specific offer.

## Headline formulas

The headline is everything. Test 3-5 versions before committing.

### 1. The promise + timeframe + proof
> "Book 12 sales calls in 30 days — without paid ads"

Pattern: `[specific outcome] in [specific timeframe] — [without the obvious downside]`

### 2. The contrarian
> "Stop chasing leads. This playbook converts the ones you already have."

Pattern: `Stop [common doomed action]. [New approach] [specific result].`

### 3. The "for [tribe]" identity
> "For agency owners who hate ad-spend roulette."

Pattern: `For [specific tribe] who [shared pain in their language].`

### 4. The disqualifier-as-headline (works for warm/qualified traffic)
> "This is not for people who want to make money on the internet."

Pattern: Set the bar high. The audience that self-identifies as worthy gets pulled in.

### 5. The before/after
> "From $0 to $34K MRR in 90 days. Here's exactly how."

Pattern: `From [starting point] to [outcome] in [timeframe]. [Promise to reveal mechanism].`

### 6. The named mechanism
> "The KFC Method: How we turn cold strangers into $5K clients in under 14 days."

Pattern: `The [Memorable Named Method]: How [we/you] [outcome] in [timeframe].`

A named mechanism creates ownable IP and makes the offer feel like it's not generic.

### 7. The negation of the dream
> "Getting more leads isn't going to save your business."

Pattern: Tell the audience what they think the answer is, then say it's wrong. (Used in Voics/Apex VSL.)

### 8. The identity-crisis reframe (Client Ascension)
> "Most online businesses trade your time for money. More clients = more work = more hours = no freedom. You're just creating another job for yourself."

Pattern: Take the audience's current goal and reveal it as the trap they're already in. Drops directly above the offer reveal — when the visitor's defenses are highest. Reframes the entire category as the problem and your offer as the only exit.

Use it mid-page, NOT in the hero. The hero promises the outcome; the identity-crisis reframe shows them they've been chasing the wrong outcome the whole time.

## Subheadline formulas

The subheadline answers "and what specifically does that mean?"

- `[Headline]. [Specific mechanism in 1 sentence]. [Proof.]`
- `The [adjective] approach [tribe] uses to [outcome] — without [downside 1], [downside 2], or [downside 3].`

## CTA button verbs (ranked)

Strong, action-specific, low-cognitive-load. In rough order of conversion lift:

| Strong | Mediocre | Banned |
|--------|----------|--------|
| Apply now | Get started | Click here |
| Book my call | Sign up | Submit |
| Reserve my seat | Learn more | Continue |
| Show me the plan | Subscribe | OK |
| Get instant access | Join | Next |
| Yes — I'm in | Register | → |

Better: make it first-person. "Yes — book my strategy call" beats "Book a call".

## Sub-CTA fine print (under the button)

This line removes friction. Examples:

- "No credit card required."
- "Takes 60 seconds. No spam, ever."
- "30-day money-back guarantee, no questions asked."
- "Only 12 seats remaining in this cohort."
- "Free instant access. Watch on your phone, laptop, or tablet."

## Objection-handling phrases

Stick these into copy where the visitor's brain is about to object:

- **"You might be thinking…"** then state their objection in their words, then dismantle it.
- **"This is not another…"** then list what people fear they'll get ("another $97 course", "another mastermind that wastes your weekends", etc.).
- **"Here's the thing nobody tells you…"** creates curiosity + frames you as insider.
- **"If you've tried [common alternative] and it didn't work, it's because…"** validates failure + positions your mechanism as the missing piece.

## Pain agitation patterns

Don't just name the pain — make it specific and visceral.

| Generic | Specific & visceral |
|---------|---------------------|
| You're not making enough money | You're checking Stripe at 11pm hoping a charge came in |
| Leads are hard to find | You spent $4,300 on ads last month and got 6 booked calls — 2 showed up |
| Hard to scale | You're the bottleneck. Every $1 of revenue needs an hour of your time |
| Burnout | You haven't taken a real weekend in 14 months |

## Risk-reversal language

Quantify the guarantee. Make it specific and absurd-feeling.

- "If you don't [outcome] in [timeframe], I'll [refund + extra]."
- "If at any point you decide this isn't for you, you can leave with no penalty and keep the bonuses."
- "We'll work with you 1:1 until you [outcome]. If that takes 12 months instead of 90 days, we don't charge more."
- "Conditional guarantee: if we don't produce the deliverable, you don't pay."

## Urgency language (real urgency only)

- "Cohort starts [exact date]. Doors close [exact date]."
- "[Specific number] seats remaining. When they're gone, the next cohort opens [later date]."
- "Founding member pricing ends [date]. Price doubles after."

Never: "limited time only" with no specific deadline. Visitors are conditioned to ignore it.

## Testimonial structure

A great testimonial has 4 parts:

1. **Identity** — Name, role, company, photo
2. **Before state** — Where they were ("Struggling to land clients while juggling a full-time job")
3. **Specific result** — Number + timeframe ("Went from $0 to $18K/month in 4 months")
4. **One line of texture** — A surprising detail that makes it feel real ("I closed two deals from a single LinkedIn DM template he gave me")

If a testimonial is missing any of these, push the client to get the missing piece.

## The "Who this is for / not for" pattern

Side-by-side qualifier. Used by Apex, Client Ascension, Passionate Few.

```
THIS IS FOR YOU IF:                     THIS IS NOT FOR YOU IF:
• You run an established business       • You're looking for a get-rich-quick scheme
• You already have a working offer      • You need someone else to do the work for you
• You're willing to follow a system     • You can't commit 5 hours per week
• You can invest in your growth         • You think "tactics" matter more than "strategy"
```

This pattern simultaneously qualifies leads AND creates desire (visitors want to be on the "for me" side).

## The price-anchored offer stack

Each line is a deliverable + its standalone "what this would cost" anchor. Builds value, then drops a price that feels obvious.

```
Module 1: The Foundation                      (Value: $2,997)
Module 2: The Outreach System                 (Value: $4,997)
Module 3: The Sales Conversation Framework    (Value: $1,997)
Bonus: Weekly 1:1 with our team               (Value: $9,997)
Bonus: Done-for-you templates library         (Value: $1,497)
                                              ─────────────
                                              Total Value: $21,485

Your investment today: $4,997
```

The trick: every line in the stack must be a real, distinct deliverable — not "lifetime access" or "community" (those feel like padding).

## Headline punch-up rules

After writing a headline, run it through this filter:

1. **Specificity** — Replace every vague word with a specific number, name, or timeframe.
2. **Cut throat-clearing** — Delete "Discover how to", "Learn the secret to", "The ultimate guide to". Start at the verb or the outcome.
3. **Front-load the payoff** — The most important word should be in the first 4 words.
4. **Read it out loud** — If you stumble, rewrite. If a 12-year-old wouldn't get it, simplify.
5. **Pass the so-what test** — After saying the headline, ask "so what?". If there's no obvious answer, the headline isn't doing its job.
